What Is Unsupervised Learning?

Unsupervised learning is a type of Machine Learning in which an algorithm studies unlabeled data. There is no target column telling the model the correct answer.

Instead of learning from examples such as “this image is a cat,” the model searches for repeated patterns, similarities, differences, and relationships. The result helps us understand the organization of the data.

This approach is especially useful during exploration, when the important relationships are not known yet. It can help analysts discover natural segments, summarize complex datasets, and generate questions for later analysis.

Core idea Give the algorithm observations without answers and let it discover meaningful structure.

What Is Clustering?

Grouping similar observations together.

Clustering is an unsupervised learning task that divides observations into groups called clusters. Items in the same cluster should be more similar to one another than to items in other clusters.

Similarity depends on the features selected and on the distance measure used by the algorithm. For example, customers may be grouped by spending and visit frequency, while documents may be grouped by the words and topics they contain.

Similarity

A distance measure determines which observations are close to each other.

Points with similar feature values tend to receive the same cluster assignment.

Groups

The algorithm creates groups from the patterns it finds in the data.

A cluster is useful when its members share a meaningful characteristic.

Insight

The groups can reveal segments, behaviors, or unusual observations.

The final interpretation comes from connecting each group to the real-world problem.

How Does K-Means Work?

A repeated process that assigns points to the nearest center.

K-Means is a popular clustering algorithm. The letter K represents the number of clusters we want to create. The algorithm uses a representative point for each cluster and repeatedly improves its position.

K-Means tries to keep points close to the center of their assigned cluster. It does this by alternating between a decision step, where points are assigned, and a correction step, where the centers are recalculated from the current assignments.

1

Choose K

Decide how many clusters the data should have.

K is a modeling decision and can affect the final interpretation.

2

Place centroids

Start with one center for each cluster.

These initial positions provide the starting point for the first assignment.

3

Assign points

Send every point to its nearest centroid.

The nearest center is selected by comparing the distances to all centroids.

4

Move centers

Recalculate each centroid and repeat.

Moving the centers allows the clusters to better represent their assigned points.

The Role of Centroids

A centroid is the center or average position of the observations currently assigned to a cluster.

For every feature, K-Means calculates the mean value of all points in that cluster. In two dimensions, the centroid is the average of the x-coordinates and the average of the y-coordinates.

After points are reassigned, the centroid may move because the membership of its cluster has changed. This movement is the mechanism that gradually improves the grouping.

ImportantA centroid is not necessarily an original data point. It is a calculated representative location.

Iterations and Convergence

Why K-Means repeats the same two operations.

One iteration consists of assigning points to centroids and then updating the centroids. The assignments change at first, but they usually become more stable after several iterations.

The process is not learning from a correct answer; it is optimizing an internal objective: keeping the total distance between each point and its assigned centroid as small as possible.

Assignment stepNearest center

Measure the distance from each observation to every centroid. Assign the observation to the closest one.

Update stepNew average

For each cluster, calculate the mean of its assigned points. That mean becomes the new centroid.

K-Means stops when the centroids barely move, the assignments stop changing, or a maximum number of iterations is reached. This stable state is called convergence.

A Complete K-Means Example

Imagine grouping customers by annual spending and visit frequency.

This example shows how an abstract mathematical result can become a practical segmentation. The two input features describe each customer, while the final clusters provide a compact way to compare their behavior.

Step 1

Choose K = 3

We decide to create three customer groups: occasional, regular, and frequent customers.

The choice should be supported by domain knowledge or a comparison of different K values.

Step 2

Assign customers

Each customer is assigned to the nearest of the three initial centroids.

A customer with high spending and frequent visits will tend to move toward the corresponding cluster center.

Step 3

Repeat and interpret

After convergence, examine the final groups and give them meaningful business names.

The names are interpretations added by people; K-Means itself only returns assignments and centroids.

Remember: K-Means discovers mathematical groups. The human analyst interprets what those groups mean in the real world.
